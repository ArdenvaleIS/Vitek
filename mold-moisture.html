Mold keeps coming back? Find out why.

Find the root cause. Avoid unnecessary costs.

Mold can grow when building materials remain damp. Cleaning can remove visible growth without correcting the moisture conditions that allowed it to develop.

I investigate why moisture accumulates in walls, ceilings, attics and crawl spaces, and recommend practical ways to reduce damp areas and the risk of mold returning.

Moisture control is key to mold management

Water may enter the building directly, or moisture in humid air may condense on a sufficiently cold surface.

Preventing unwanted water entry, managing humid air and avoiding cold surfaces help keep building materials dry.

Think of a house as a fortress: the first line of defense is to keep the enemy (water) outside, rather than allow it into the house and then try to remove it.

Three conditions I investigate

These conditions can occur separately or interact. Identifying which ones are present is more useful than assuming every mold problem has the same cause.

1 · Water entry

Water entering the building

Water can enter through roof or flashing leaks, plumbing leaks, windows, exterior walls, poor drainage or soil moisture reaching a crawl space.

The source needs to be identified and corrected; repeated drying alone may not resolve the problem.

2 · Air movement

Air moving through the building envelope (walls and ceilings)

Air can move through gaps, penetrations and poorly sealed areas. Depending on conditions, humid air can carry moisture into colder parts of walls, ceilings, attics or crawl spaces, where condensation may occur.

Air sealing must be considered together with ventilation and moisture management for the particular part of the building. Sealing gaps without understanding how air and moisture move can create other problems.

3 · Cold surfaces

Cold surfaces where moisture can condense

When humid air contacts a surface below the air's dew-point temperature, water vapor can condense into liquid water. A bathroom mirror or window after a shower is a familiar example.

Similar conditions can occur at thermal bridges—areas where heat passes through framing or other building materials more readily—or where insulation is missing or poorly fitted. Persistent condensation can leave materials damp.

Insulation: material quality is not enough

Properly selected and installed insulation helps slow heat transfer and keep surfaces warmer, reducing the risk of condensation. But insulation is only one part of moisture control; water entry, air movement and ventilation also matter.

Even good materials, such as mineral wool (including Rockwool), cannot compensate for poor fitting or installation.

What an independent assessment can cover

What I can review

  • Visual assessment of accessible areas and moisture-related conditions
  • Potential water-entry points, drainage and crawl-space conditions
  • Humidity, temperature and moisture measurements where appropriate
  • Air leakage, ventilation, HVAC operation and possible condensation conditions
  • Insulation installation and visible thermal bridges where accessible
  • Proposed remediation, insulation or ventilation work
  • A written summary of findings, photographs and prioritized recommendations

What I do not provide

  • Mold removal, cleaning, remediation or reconstruction
  • Laboratory mold testing, mold-species identification or medical assessments
  • Opening walls or other invasive investigations
  • Plumbing, roofing, electrical, HVAC or other trade repairs
  • Engineering designs or services requiring a professional license
  • A guarantee that mold will never return

Some situations require specialists for laboratory testing, plumbing, roofing, structural investigation, remediation or medical advice. I will explain when such expertise is needed.
